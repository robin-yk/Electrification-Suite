Curvature · Λ and Da ratios

Rate, transport, and velocity curvature, compared

Curvature determines whether pulsing provides a benefit

A pulsed element spends a fraction d of each cycle at Tpeak and the rest at Tmin, so the time-average of any property f sits on the straight chord between its two endpoint values, while a continuous heater at the same average temperature gives the curve value f(Tavg). Chord above curve (convex, like the Arrhenius rate k): pulsing wins. Chord below (concave): pulsing loses. The sign of f″ is all that matters.

The β slider is a stress test, not a tuning knob. The Λ advantage requires h(T) concave (0 < β < 1); if gas-phase diffusion dominates, β exceeds 1 (diffusivity alone scales roughly as T1.75) and part of the gain cancels. Check which exponent describes your reactor before trusting the Λ result.

k(T): convex

chord above

⟨k⟩ exceeds k(Tavg) whenever Ea > 2RT; the resulting gap represents the pulsing gain.

Transfer response h(T): concave

chord below

⟨h⟩ falls below h(Tavg) for β < 1, reducing the denominator of Λ.

u(T): linear

chord on curve

⟨u⟩ equals u(Tavg) exactly; pulsing provides no benefit on the convective timescale.

Duty-cycle dependence

Low duty cycle lowers Tavg, so the ratio against that cooler reference rises even as absolute throughput falls, which the ratio does not capture.

The ratio alone does not determine practical benefit. It compares RPH against a cooler continuous heater held at the RPH average temperature, not against continuous operation at Tpeak, the comparison relevant when power is not limiting. There the case for pulsing rests on selectivity and element lifetime, not on Λ or Da.

Explore: three things to try

Stretch the period. The hot window τp = d P grows past 1/k2(Tpeak) and the B yield collapses.

Kill the separation. Set Ea,2 = Ea,1 and k2 = k1: pulsing now loses, because everything made in the pulse burns on to C before it can flow out.

Raise the duty cycle. Conversion rises but B sits in the hot window longer, so selectivity falls. Frequency is the stronger lever.

The two conditions

Formation completes in the pulse: k1(Tpeak) · τp ≫ 1—
B survives the pulse: k2(Tpeak) · τp < 1—

A continuous heater cannot satisfy both at once: hot enough to form B means hot for B too, all the time. Supporting numbers: selectivity leverage k1/k2 at Tpeak = — (set by Ea,1 > Ea,2); washout between pulses (1 − d) P/τ = —.

Model and references

The other tabs compare a single rate. Many real targets, though, are intermediates: in pulsed methane pyrolysis the value sits in C2 products, which the same heat that makes them then converts to aromatics and coke. In a series network A → B → C, a short hot pulse lets the fast first step run, then quenches before the slower second step consumes what was just made, while flow carries B out during the cold fraction. The two reactors above run that minimal model live, two first-order steps in a CSTR: pulsed on the left, continuous on the right, same feed.

The model is an isothermal CSTR with two first-order homogeneous steps and an imposed temperature program: the same minimal idealization Railkar & Vlachos used to rationalize pulsed methane pyrolysis (Ind. Eng. Chem. Res. 2024, 10.1021/acs.iecr.3c03198). The experimental case is Dong et al. (Nature 2022, 10.1038/s41586-022-04568-6): CH4 → C2 → aromatics/coke, where 0.02–0.055 s pulses reached ~80% C2 selectivity against <35% for the non-catalytic continuous control.

Nothing here is fit to those data; the tab visualizes the two conditions, not the numbers. Reactor thermal mass, gas thermal lag, and transport limits are ignored, and in physical-drive mode the energy balance applies to the element only. Equations are recorded in the Calculations tab.

Equations, assumptions, and dimensional cancellation

All temperatures are converted to kelvin before entering any Arrhenius or power-law expression. The page reports ratios, so the pre-exponential A and the reference group h0/T0β cancel identically and are never needed as inputs.
Primary reference: Kwak, Y., Railkar, R., Zheng, W., & Vlachos, D. G. (2025). Tandem Nonoxidative Coupling of Methane and Carbon Dioxide Reduction via Pulsed Joule Thermochemistry. ACS Energy Letters, 10(12), 6188–6196. https://doi.org/10.1021/acsenergylett.5c02853. This page implements the RPH-vs-CJH dimensionless analysis developed there.

Constitutive forms

Rate constant
k(T) = A exp(−Ea / RT)
k″(T) = k(T) · Ea(Ea − 2RT) / (R2T4), so k is convex whenever Ea > 2RT. Default Ea = 100 kJ/mol; the lumped first-order value for homogeneous methane pyrolysis (2CH4 → C2H2 + 3H2) is ≈422 kJ/mol and can be dialed in with the slider.
Mass-transfer coefficient
h(T) = h0 (T / T0)β
h″(T) < 0 for 0 < β < 1. The slider spans past 1 so the concavity assumption can be stress-tested rather than assumed.
Velocity
u(T) = u0 (T / T0)
Ideal gas at fixed pressure and fixed molar flow. u″ = 0, so its time-average is exactly its average-temperature value.
Relative exposure
Rexposure = ⟨k(T)/u(T)⟩ / [k(Tavg)/u(Tavg)]
Residence time matters for conversion, but the page deliberately does not report absolute Da without a kinetic prefactor and a reactor model.

Time-averaging

Continuous integration (Kinetic Effect tab)
⟨f⟩ = (1/t) ∫ f(T(t')) dt'
Accumulated numerically each animation frame over the active waveform: the computed CFP solution in physical mode, or the ideal trapezoid including its ramps. This is what the running-average charts plot.
Ideal two-state limit (ratios tab)
⟨f⟩ = d f(Tpeak) + (1 − d) f(Tmin)
Recovered when the ramp fraction goes to zero. This is the form the note proves against.
Reference temperature
Tavg = d Tpeak + (1 − d) Tmin
The CJH comparison is pinned here by default, so the two systems share a time-average temperature, unless the physical drive's equal-average-power reference is selected.
The two ratios
RΛ = ⟨kL/h⟩ / [k(Tavg)L/h(Tavg)]
Rexposure = ⟨k/u⟩ / [k(Tavg)/u(Tavg)]
The ratios are dimensionless because the common characteristic length L cancels. The instantaneous k/u ratio is averaged directly; ⟨k/u⟩ cannot in general be replaced by ⟨k⟩/⟨u⟩.

Physical CFP drive (Kinetic Effect tab option)

Lumped element energy balance
m cp(T) dT/dt = V2/R(T) · pulse(t) − εσA(T4 − T∞4) − ĊHe(T − Tin)
Integrated with RK4 over whole pulse cycles until the cycle-start temperature repeats (periodic steady state). A single temperature is justified by the element's measured spatial uniformity (companion-paper SI, Scheme S2) and its 210 μm thickness.
Element and parameters
R(T) = 4.22 − 7.24×10−4 T  Ω;   A = 2 × 38 × 8 mm2;   m = 28.8 mg;   ε = 0.57
Freudenberg H23 carbon fiber paper from the companion-paper SI: measured resistance line (its Fig. S11a), strip geometry and mass, and the IR-calibrated surface emissivity. cp(T) uses a graphite table capped near the Dulong–Petit limit; He pickup uses the 50 sccm capacity rate with the gas leaving at element temperature.
CJH references
V2/R(TCJH) = losses(TCJH)   or   P̄ = losses(TCJH)
The comparison panel can hold CJH at the RPH time-average temperature (the analysis convention) or at the steady temperature that consumes the same average electrical power (the experimental convention, the paper's Scheme S1f). Steady solutions come from bisection on the same loss terms.
What this adds, and what it still omits
Asymmetric rise and radiative-decay waveform; amplitude collapse when the period is shorter than m cp/G.
Contact and lead conduction default to zero (lumpable via a conductance parameter in the solver); coke deposition, element ageing, and gas thermal lag remain outside the model, as in the ideal mode. Below 500 °C the IR camera cannot see the element, so that part of the trace is model prediction only.

Series network A → B → C (its own tab)

Species balances, CSTR with pure-A feed
dxA/dt = (1 − xA)/τ − k1(T) xA
dxB/dt = k1(T) xA − xB/τ − k2(T) xB
Isothermal fixed-mass CSTR, both steps first-order and homogeneous, T(t) imposed from the active waveform: the ideal trapezoid or the computed CFP solution. xC = 1 − xA − xB.
Rate constants
ki(T) = ki,ref exp[−Ea,i/R (1/T − 1/Tref)],   Tref = 1100 °C
Anchoring at Tref makes the sliders physical: ki,ref is the rate constant you would measure at 1100 °C, and Ea,i sets how fast it climbs from there. Defaults: Ea,1 = 400 kJ/mol (near the lumped methane-pyrolysis value the constitutive-forms card cites) against a flat Ea,2 = 80 kJ/mol, with k1,ref/k2,ref = 30.
Periodic state, solved exactly
xend = M xstart + c  ⇒  x* = (I − M)−1 c
The balances are linear, so each time step has an exact exponential update and one cycle composes to an affine map whose fixed point is the periodic state: two passes over the cycle, stable no matter how large k(Tpeak) becomes. The reported B yield is the cycle-average outlet fraction ⟨xB⟩; the CJH value is the analytic steady solution at the reference temperature.
Provenance
Minimal network of Railkar & Vlachos (2024); experimental case Dong et al. (2022).
The CSTR analysis (10.1021/acs.iecr.3c03198) reduced programmable pulsed pyrolysis to this two-step model; the tab reuses that reduction with the page's own waveforms and CJH references. Defaults are illustrative, not fitted.

Assumptions carried, and their cost

Single lumped rate
One Ea stands in for the whole radical network.
This is why the page never converts Da into a predicted conversion. Methane coupling is chain-initiated with an induction period, and a lumped k significantly overpredicts conversion at millisecond contact times.
Gas follows the element instantly
Tgas = Telement with no lag.
A real pulse shorter than the gas thermal time constant is filtered, and the gas sees a smaller swing than the element. Short periods on the slider are therefore optimistic.
β treated as constant
One exponent across 400 to 1400 °C.
Flagged rather than defended. See the Λ caveat on the Jensen tab.
No coke, no element ageing
Properties are time-invariant.
Deposition and element degradation are the actual limits on a pulsed CFP heater and sit entirely outside this model.
